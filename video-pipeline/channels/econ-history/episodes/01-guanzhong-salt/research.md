## 1. Context — who, when, where

- **Guan Zhong 管仲** (管夷吾, c. 720–645 BC), chancellor of the state of **Qi 齐** (Shandong peninsula) under
  **Duke Huan 齐桓公** (r. 685–643 BC). ESTABLISHED. [Wikipedia: Guan Zhong; Qi (state)]
- Qi had just been through years of succession conflict; the treasury was depleted. ESTABLISHED (broad outline).
- **The arrow story**: in the race for the throne (685 BC) Guan Zhong backed Prince Jiu 公子纠 and shot the rival,
  Prince Xiaobai 公子小白, hitting his belt hook (带钩); Xiaobai feigned death, reached Qi first and became Duke Huan.
  Source: 《史记·齐太公世家》; also told in the *Zuozhuan* tradition. TRADITIONAL (recorded centuries later, but
  universally repeated).
- **Bao Shuya 鲍叔牙** persuaded the new duke to spare and employ Guan Zhong. 《史记·齐太公世家》 has Bao say, in
  substance: to govern Qi, Gao Xi and I suffice; to become hegemon, you need Guan Yiwu
  (“君将治齐，即高傒与叔牙足也。君且欲霸王，非管夷吾不可。” **[verify]**). TRADITIONAL.
- **Results**: Duke Huan became the first of the “Five Hegemons” 春秋五霸; 667 BC interstate covenant with Qi as
  leader. 《论语·宪问》: “桓公九合诸侯，不以兵车，管仲之力也” and “微管仲，吾其被发左衽矣”. ESTABLISHED (text).
- Confucius also criticised Guan Zhong's extravagance and lack of ritual propriety (《论语·八佾》 “管仲之器小哉…焉得俭？…
  管氏而知礼，孰不知礼？”). ESTABLISHED (text).

## 2. The idea — 官山海 “put the mountains and seas under state control”

Source: 《管子·海王》 (Guanzi ch. 72 “King of the Sea”), a dialogue between Duke Huan and Guan Zhong. Search-verified
fragments:

- The duke proposes taxing buildings, trees, livestock and people; Guan Zhong rejects each as harmful or resented.
- “唯官山海为可耳” — only putting the mountains and seas under state management will do.
- “海王之国，谨正盐筴” — a state that is “king of the sea” carefully manages its salt policy.
- Monthly consumption: “终月，大男食盐五升少半，大女食盐三升少半，吾子食盐二升少半” — per month an adult man eats
  5⅓ *sheng* of salt, an adult woman 3⅓, a child 2⅓.
- Price arithmetic: “令盐之重升加分强，釜五十也；升加一强，釜百也；升加二强，釜二百也…” — raise the price by a
  fraction of a coin per *sheng* and each *fu* (100 *sheng*) yields 50 coins; one coin per *sheng* yields 100; etc.
- Scale: “万乘之国，人数开口千万也” (a large state has ten million mouths); a head tax of “月人三十钱之籍” (30 coins
  per person per month) would raise 30 million, whereas the salt markup brings in “二国之籍者六千万” — the revenue of
  two such states, 60 million — without taxing anyone directly.
- Iron: “今铁官之数曰：一女必有一针一刀…耕者必有一耒一耜一铫…” — every woman needs a needle and a knife, every
  farmer a plough, a spade and a hoe; a small markup on each tool reaches the whole population. **[verify wording]**

Mechanism (in modern terms): a state trading monopoly on a necessity with **price-inelastic demand**, collecting a
hidden markup — functionally a consumption tax, levied through the price rather than by a tax collector. The text's
own selling point is political: people resent visible taxes, not prices.

## 3. Authorship and dating — critical caveat

- The received *Guanzi* was edited by the Han scholar **Liu Xiang 刘向** c. 26 BC from many earlier materials
  (86 chapters, 76 extant). ESTABLISHED.
- The **Qingzhong 轻重 chapters** (incl. 海王, 国蓄) are generally dated to the Warring States–Western Han period, not
  to Guan Zhong. Kanaya Osamu (1984) places their compilation between Emperors Wen/Jing and the end of Emperor Wu's
  reign; written by a group of economic advisers (“轻重家”). Rickett's translation notes centuries of accretion.
  DEBATED (exact date), but scholars agree they are not Guan Zhong's own words.
- Therefore: **did the historical Guan Zhong run a salt monopoly?** The *Shiji* credits him with exploiting
  “the profits of fish and salt” (《齐太公世家》 “设轻重鱼盐之利，以赡贫穷，禄贤能” **[verify]**) and with
  “通货积财，富国强兵，与俗同好恶” (《管晏列传》, search-verified). That supports state involvement in salt; the
  full monopoly-by-markup design is best presented as the *Guanzi* school's model attributed to him. DEBATED.

## 5. Consequences and legacy

- **Han**: under Emperor Wu, financially strained by wars against the Xiongnu, salt and iron became state
  monopolies from c. 119 BC, run by former salt tycoon Dongguo Xianyang 东郭咸阳 and iron magnate Kong Jin 孔仅, with
  Sang Hongyang 桑弘羊. 《史记·平准书》《汉书·食货志》. ESTABLISHED (year given as 119–117 BC in different accounts).
- **Salt and Iron Debate 盐铁会议, 81 BC** (始元六年): Sang Hongyang vs. 60-odd Confucian “worthies and literati”
  贤良文学; recorded/edited by Huan Kuan 桓宽 as 《盐铁论》. Critics: the state competes with the people for profit
  (与民争利); state iron tools were poor and expensive. ESTABLISHED.
- **Tang**: monopoly re-imposed by Diwu Qi 第五琦 in 758 (乾元元年) after the An Lushan rebellion; reformed by Liu Yan
  刘晏. Salt became a pillar of revenue. ESTABLISHED. Rebel leader **Huang Chao 黄巢** came from a salt-smuggling
  family (《新唐书·黄巢传》). ESTABLISHED.
- **Modern China**: State Council reform plan (2016) took effect 1 Jan 2017 — prices and cross-region sales
  liberalised; production still licensed. ESTABLISHED. Headlines called it the end of a “2,000-year monopoly”; that
  is journalistic shorthand, not a continuous institution.

## 6. Western comparisons (labelled as analogies)

- **France's gabelle**: abolished in 1790 during the Revolution, reinstated by Napoleon in 1806, ended 1945/46
  (sources differ by a year). ESTABLISHED.
- **Gandhi's Salt March**: 12 March – 6 April 1930, ~387 km (240 miles) to Dandi, defying British salt laws in India.
  ESTABLISHED.
- Modern economics: excise taxes on goods with inelastic demand (tobacco, fuel) — same logic. ANALOGY.

## 7. Myths to avoid

- “Guan Zhong wrote the Guanzi” — no; see §3.
- “China had a single salt monopoly for 2,000 years” — no; policies changed repeatedly (Tang early period had no
  monopoly; forms varied between taxes, licensing and direct sale).
- Treating the *Guanzi*'s numbers as census or budget data — they are illustrative.

## 8. Comedic material

- The chancellor who once shot his boss; the duke who “played dead”; Bao Shuya's legendary loyalty (管鲍之交).
- The text reads like a modern consultant's pitch deck: “No new taxes!” — plus a spreadsheet.
- Confucius: “small-minded and extravagant… but without him we'd all be barbarians.”
